# Learn what this audience already watches

A marketing script works when its viewers find something they want, in a shape they already know, and then meet something new. One reference video says little about what an audience enjoys. Before choosing a direction, read or build the brand's record of what this audience longs for and already watches.

## Where it lives

The record belongs to the project's brand layer described in [records](../../mixreel/references/records.md), shared by every film for this brand.

- `brand/taste-library/` holds one folder per analyzed example, with the video or a link, frames, transcript and the breakdown below.
- `brand/audience-taste.md` holds what this audience longs for, the familiar elements they know and the reasons they keep watching, each pointing to its examples.
- `brand/competitors.md` holds how each competitor advertises and what its audience reacts to.

When these exist, read them and check their dates. When they are missing, build them as below and show the summary to the user before writing a script from it. When the user corrects what the audience wants, write the correction into `brand/audience-taste.md`.

## Gather evidence of taste

Use what the advertiser supplies first, such as examples they admire, earlier campaign results, social research and an existing taste record. Add evidence the available tools can reach.

- Ads in the same category that have kept running for weeks in public ad libraries. Long running time is weak evidence that the advertiser keeps paying for them; it cannot rank ads that started on the same day or show which element worked. Ads that reuse the same footage under different copy count once.
- Popular posts this audience makes or shares, and the language in their comments.
- Songs, sounds and formats currently circulating among them.

When no source is reachable, ask the user for examples or access rather than inventing taste from a demographic label. Record each source and its date; taste moves quickly.

## Break each example down

For each example, write what happens on screen with times and exact text, then answer these questions.

- Why does this viewer keep watching? Name the concrete moment and what it gives them, such as recognition of their own life, a laugh, curiosity about what happens next, a fantasy coming true, a real person's reaction, the urge to step in and choose, or sensory pleasure.
- What does the viewer long for here, and how does the ad deliver it?
- What familiar thing does it borrow, and what does it add that the viewer has not seen?
- How does the product enter, and what does the viewer learn it can do?

Identify songs and sounds by name. A transcript gives only fragments of lyrics; search them, and mark a song unidentified rather than guessing. Group the examples by the reason viewers keep watching, then note which reasons the category overuses.

A breakdown at the expected depth, from a roleplay chat app's ad that ran for over three months. In flat webtoon panels a tall girl tells a shorter boy look up, baby boy. A caption says one year later; he is now a head taller, leans down and asks who's looking up now? A drawn hand holds a phone that says the story continues in the app, followed by painted intimate shots.

- **Why viewers stay.** A complete reversal lands within six seconds, and the first frames read as a webtoon meme, not an ad.
- **Longing.** The boy she teased grows up and turns the tables on her.
- **Familiar and new.** The tall girlfriend, short boyfriend meme and the webtoon look; the new part is that the story goes on in the app.
- **Product.** A drawn phone at seven seconds and an end card.

## Write down what they long for

From the breakdowns, write the few desires that keep recurring, in the audience's own terms, with the examples that show each. Write the pains they live with too, since a film may open on one, and note what turns each pain into the desire. This list is where every idea in [marketing idea](marketing-idea.md) starts.

## Choose what to borrow

Familiar material can come from many places. These are examples, not a checklist.

- Well-known characters, franchises and celebrities
- Songs and sounds already circulating
- Holidays, seasons and other moments on the calendar
- Current events and conversations online
- Situations and stereotypes the audience jokes about
- Visual styles and genre conventions they recognize at a glance
- Products and apps they already use, as a point of comparison
- Meme formats and caption patterns

Borrow something the evidence shows this audience knows. Pair it with one new element, usually the product experience the brief wants remembered. A script built only from familiar parts feels like a copy; a script with nothing familiar gives the viewer nothing to hold on to.

Some familiar material belongs to others. Record the rights position of each borrowed element. Commercial music, characters, trademarks and real people need permission or a licensable route, such as a platform's commercial sound library or an archetype that evokes a character without copying protected features. Present that choice and its trade-off to the user; do not silently drop a strong idea or silently use protected material.
