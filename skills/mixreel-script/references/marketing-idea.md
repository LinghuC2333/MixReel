# Shape the idea viewers stay for

Use this for marketing scripts, after the project's `brand/audience-taste.md` record, built with [audience taste](audience-taste.md), has named what this audience already knows and wants. It covers how the idea is built and staged. What the film may claim and how its product UI is realized belong to [advertising message](ad-script.md).

## Begin from what the audience longs for

A good marketing script gives this audience a reason to keep watching from the first frame to the last, and leaves them knowing what the product lets them do. Build every idea in three steps.

1. Write down what this audience longs for, from the taste record, in their terms. It is usually a feeling or an outcome they want for themselves, such as being chosen, being proven right or getting even.
2. Find the scene, the characters and the story moment that deliver that longing. Prefer a moment where it is about to be won or lost.
3. Build that moment from elements the audience already knows, such as tropes, formats, characters, songs and situations from their own lives.

An idea at the expected depth, for an interactive romance app whose feature lets the viewer type a line into the story. The taste record says this audience longs for a dangerous man everyone fears who makes an exception only for her. At a masquerade, guests bow and step aside as the most feared vampire in the city walks through. He stops only in front of her and says you shouldn't be here. The viewer holds his line and types then make me leave. The roll succeeds and she says it to his face. His eyes turn red, he tells the room everyone out, and a hundred guests flee. She turns to leave as well; he catches her wrist and says not you. An earlier draft had him pretend not to know her. It was familiar and easy to read, but a man who ignores her is only a bad boyfriend, so it gave the audience nothing they wanted.

Reasons to keep watching differ, such as recognizing their own life, laughing, wanting to know what happens next, seeing a fantasy come true, watching a real person react, wanting to step in and choose, or sensory pleasure. Take the reason from the taste record. Do not default to the emotion the category already crowds; when every candidate idea relies on the same reason, return to the record.

Choose a format that fits the idea, such as a demonstration, a meme skit, a prank, a conversation, a subjective viewpoint or a continuous scene. Do not force every ad through a fixed problem, solution, twist and call-to-action template. If the advertiser wants a pure emotional teaser, keep that choice and show how it supports the goal.

## Familiar and attractive

Familiar alone does not hold anyone. Check that the situation carries the pull this audience seeks, such as attraction and tension, status, comfort or revenge, as the taste record shows. A situation the audience knows only as a pain, such as being ignored or let down, can open the film, but the payoff turns it into what they desire. A film that ends where the pain began only repeats their life back to them. Combine familiar parts into a new chain of escalation; the parts make it readable, the chain makes it fresh.

## Start inside the feeling

Open at the moment the emotion is already in play. Skip the discovery, introduction or setup that would lead to it. A viewer who sees a character react, such as dropping a glass and walking out, already knows how she feels and wants to know what happens next. When the first frame holds the conflict, the opening needs no separate trick.

A viewer meets a short ad knowing nothing about its characters. Choose a moment whose situation reads from the picture and one line of text. Each piece of background the moment needs, such as a name, a past event or a rule of the story world, costs screen time and attention. When a moment needs more than one, choose another moment.

## Let actions carry the story

Build the film from visible actions, each one causing the next. Keep dialogue to the few lines only a voice can deliver, and let each line do something, such as refusing, teasing or daring. Write it as a real person would say it in that moment, short and plain. Formal declarations, recited rules, full-name speeches and characters reciting product features sound childish on screen.

## Escalate every beat

Make each event larger than the one before it. The chain of escalation is what keeps viewers watching; the first second only starts it. When two beats carry the same weight, cut one or raise the second.

## Put the choice at the peak

When the product lets the viewer choose, speak or act, place that moment where the emotion peaks and the viewer most wants to step in. Offer something this audience genuinely wants to do there, according to the taste record, such as standing their ground, answering back or walking away. A shocking option the audience would never pick, or one that makes the character betray their own interest or dignity, gives the viewer nothing to want. Make the consequence large and visible so the choice matters.

## Land on what the audience wants

Let the consequence surprise, then deliver the outcome this audience longs for, with agency where they want it. Check who chooses, who acts, who bears the cost and who receives the payoff against the taste record before polishing any wording. A premise the user has rejected fails even when the format and product facts are right.

## Let the product belong to the story

Say why the product appears at that exact moment and what the viewer learns it can do. It can enter as a prop the story depends on, such as the call or message that sets off the joke; as a character's behaviour that shows what the product makes possible; or as a direct demonstration. Choose the entry that serves the reason to keep watching. A logo appended to a story that would play the same without the product is weak unless the brief asks for it. A demonstration inserted for its own sake interrupts the viewer.

When the product appears, show it the way the product really looks and behaves, with its own layout, layering and flow, and design the story so that this moment lands at the peak. Do not shrink, move or redraw a screen to fit the story, and do not cut away to it as a separate recording when the product itself shows it over the scene. The metric a message is tested on is not something the film must act out. A film for a message measured by retries does not need a failed attempt, and showing one teaches viewers to expect failure and exposes the payment step.

## Show what only video can show

Prefer events with scale and movement, such as a crowd parting, a fall, fire, glass breaking or a room emptying. Two people standing and talking could be written in a book. Give the camera something only it can deliver, and tie the spectacle to the story's cause so it means something.

## Shots and styles serve the feeling

Choose each shot scale and angle for what the viewer should feel at that moment, such as an over-the-shoulder view for being shut out or a hand close-up for being caught. Rendering style can carry meaning too. When the film moves between worlds, such as the viewer's everyday life and a story world, give each world its own style so the switch reads without words. Keep each character's emotion on one line of change; contradictory expressions in neighbouring shots confuse viewers.

## Execution that keeps viewers

- Put any framing caption, such as a POV line, on screen from the first frame. A caption that arrives seconds later misses the viewers it was meant to hold.
- Keep the music running through the film. Duck it under a decisive moment instead of stopping and restarting it; a stop and restart reads as a broken edit. Split the film between two pieces of music only for a deliberate before-and-after contrast.

## Record why it should work

For the chosen idea, record in the script the desire it serves, the familiar point and what is new, whose viewpoint the viewer takes, the first-frame stimulus, the expectation it opens, how each beat raises it and the payoff. Mark which parts come from the user, from audience evidence, from a reference and from the agent's own judgment. A demographic label alone is not evidence of a desire. State an observable creative test or an open taste question, not a self-awarded score or predicted conversion.

## Compare directions only when useful

When comparing options, give each its own desire, familiar point and reason to keep watching; a change of staging alone does not make a new direction. Write enough beats and copy to judge each, state the trade-off and recommend one. If a single script is requested, develop one without manufacturing alternatives.

## Present with reference images

Deliver the script with a reference sheet whenever images exist, saved in the film's folder. Use frames from the brand's taste library that show the intended device, the official character, costume and background art, and product screenshots. Crop small faces so their expressions read. Label each image with the shot it supports and its source.
