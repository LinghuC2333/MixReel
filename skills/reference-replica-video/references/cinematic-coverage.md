# Cinematic coverage for Scene video

Adapted from the user-authorized source IDE prompt rules. This file writes prompts only; it does not submit generation jobs. Verify the selected endpoint with [the provider contract](providers.md) before execution. Model IDs here identify source prompt families, not universal API identifiers.

Use this guide to turn the approved visible event into a short shot sequence before writing H3 syntax.

## Preserve before directing

The approved scene controls the event, action order, character positions, gaze, props, reaction, and final image. Camera coverage may reveal those facts more clearly but may not replace them. If the source explicitly says `close-up`, `cut to`, `from inside`, `through the window`, `tracking`, or another concrete camera decision, preserve it unless it creates a direct physical contradiction.

Do not collapse two locations into one convenient composition. A person seen through a window remains on the stated side of the window. A reaction that occurs after an action remains after it. A final prop or gesture must still own the final frame.

## Decide when to cut

Cut when one composition cannot clearly carry the required information. Common reasons include:

- A wide action and a small facial reaction both matter.
- The viewer must understand what one person sees and then read that person's response.
- A plot-critical prop changes state and the change would be unclear in the master shot.
- The scene moves between distinct spaces or sides of a threshold.
- A withheld touch, maintained distance, or eyeline carries the relationship beat.

Keep a continuous shot when uninterrupted time, physical distance, surveillance, mounting discomfort, or one flowing action is itself the dramatic point. A single shot is a deliberate choice, not a default.

## Direct the emotional atmosphere

Identify the scene's emotional arc, not only its dominant feeling. Mark the opening state, the source beat that changes or intensifies it, and the feeling left in the final image. If the source contains no turn, preserve a deliberately static mood. State the atmosphere once in the H3 description, then let observable channels carry it. Useful channels include shot distance, pace, stillness, gaze, breath, micro-hesitation, light, weather, environmental motion, physical sound, touch, and deliberate non-contact.

For grief, quiet duration, restrained movement, muted ambience, withheld speech, and one physical reaction usually carry more weight than repeated sadness adjectives. For romantic tension, preserve the source's consent and boundaries while emphasizing mutual awareness, charged proximity, interrupted or withheld touch, close breathing, synchronized movement, and reaction timing. Do not turn subtext into a new kiss, caress, embrace, smile, or spoken confession.

Let atmosphere shape coverage without obscuring the event. A prop state change must remain readable, and a relationship beat must still preserve who chooses, who waits, and who touches or refuses to touch.

Do a silent-frame check before finalizing the plan. The emotional change should still be legible from performance, distance, light, or composition when dialogue and sound are removed. Sound may deepen the scene but should not carry the entire emotional turn by itself.

## Make decisive actions complete

For a plot-critical physical action, show a short causal chain rather than naming the result. Establish the initial state, make the contact or applied force visible, show the transition, then hold the result long enough to register. The model should see where the hand, tool, object, and resistance are located relative to one another.

A cutting insert may show the blades enclosing the material, pressure increasing, fibers separating, tension releasing, and the suspended object falling. A lock insert may show the key seated in the keyway, the turn, the bolt click, the handle test, and the held latch. An ownership change may keep both hands and the object visible until one hand releases and the other clearly controls it.

Avoid hiding the decisive contact behind a body, cutting away before the state changes, or asking one brief insert to perform several unrelated actions. Prefer positive placement such as `both boots remain one step behind the threshold` over a bare negative such as `do not enter`.

## Two-person coverage

Begin with a master or two-shot when the viewer needs the geography. Move closer when a gaze, hesitation, refusal, or reaction changes the meaning of the exchange.

For dialogue or silent exchange, select from these tools according to the scene:

- A two-shot establishes distance, obstruction, or who controls an object.
- An over-the-shoulder shot connects one person's look to the other person's response.
- A reverse shot gives the second person equal visual subjectivity.
- A clean close-up isolates a reaction that the other person does not notice.
- A shared final frame shows the relationship after the action has changed it.

Use the 180-degree line unless the source deliberately disorients the viewer. Match eyelines, screen direction, hand positions, and object ownership through every reverse angle.

When a boundary, withheld touch, maintained distance, or control of a prop is the relationship beat, design a proof composition. Keep the relevant hands, feet, gap, threshold, obstruction, or object in the same readable frame. Do not let the final angle flatten two people onto the same plane when their separation is the point of the scene.

## Environment shots and inserts

An environment shot may establish place, weather, isolation, danger, or the direction of travel. It can also bridge two spaces. Keep it brief enough that the event still fits the requested duration.

A prop insert may reveal evidence, mark a state change, or create a transition. Examples include shears closing on rope, a key turning, hands refusing the reins, or a suitcase handle changing owners. Return from the insert with continuous positions and motion.

Do not add an empty shot or insert only to imitate a trailer. Every added image must carry information, mood tied to the setting, or a transition already supported by the source.

## Camera movement

Choose camera behavior per shot. One shot may track while a later reaction shot stays locked. Within one shot, use one principal move so the model can preserve composition and action.

Describe movement in concrete terms such as `tracks laterally beside the horse`, `slowly pushes from a two-shot into the listener's close-up`, or `remains locked on the doorway`. Avoid stacked moves that compete for the same short interval.

## Timing

Choose duration after the shot plan is complete. There is no default clip length and no fixed shot count. Let the visible event decide.

Use these ranges as estimates rather than quotas:

- A brief environment image usually needs 0.8 to 1.5 seconds.
- A clear prop insert or simple state change usually needs 1.5 to 2.5 seconds.
- A simple body action usually needs 2 to 3 seconds.
- A complex continuous action or camera move usually needs 3 to 6 seconds.
- An important silent reaction usually needs 1.5 to 3 seconds after its cause becomes clear.
- Dialogue needs its natural spoken time plus any visible pause or reaction required by the source.

Add the estimates and round up to a whole second. Use the verified minimum duration when the total is shorter. When the total exceeds the verified maximum duration, remove decorative coverage or merge only shots that remain legible; report that the event needs multiple clips if its required beats still do not fit. Choose only durations supported by the selected endpoint. Reusing one duration across unrelated scenes is not a reason to choose it.

Give each shot enough time for its action to become readable. Do not place a cut during the decisive state change unless the cut itself carries that change, such as shears closing in close-up followed by the tire landing in a wider shot.
